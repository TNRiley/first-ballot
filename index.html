First Ballot

Every lead and supporting acting Emmy nomination since 1979 — 1,573 acting careers across twelve categories. 72.5% of them never won the category they were nominated for, not once. Of the ones who did, 68% won on their very first try. The Emmys don’t reward waiting your turn — they mostly pick you fast, or never.

Built 2026-09-22 · data through the 78th Primetime Emmy Awards, September 14, 2026

At the 78th Emmys on September 14, 2026, Matthew Rhys won Lead Actor in a Comedy Series for Widow’s Bay, and the same night, Lead Actor in a Limited or Anthology Series or Movie for The Beast in Me. Coverage called it a first: nobody had won two lead-actor Emmys in the same year.

Checked against every acting nomination from 1979 on, that holds — but only with the word lead doing real work. Two other people have won two of these twelve acting categories in one night: James Earl Jones in 1991 (a lead prize and a supporting one) and Stockard Channing in 2002 (two supporting prizes). Rhys is the first to take home two Lead trophies in the same year — a real record, just a narrower one than the headline let on.

That double is a one-off, three names in forty-eight years. The pattern underneath it is not. Scroll down to browse who else has been nominated again and again — and who, unlike Rhys, never got the envelope at all.

Nominate them twice, and the odds don’t rise — they fall

Among the 27% of careers that ever won their category, this is which nomination number it happened on. Almost seven in ten who ever win, win the first time they’re up. Whatever happens after a loss, it isn’t voters warming up to you.

The longest losing streaks

Ranked by nominations in a single category with zero wins, 1979–2026. Everyone here kept getting nominated — the voters clearly liked the work — and still never won it.

Angela Lansbury holds the record by a wide margin: twelve consecutive Lead Actress–Drama nominations for Murder, She Wrote, 1985 through 1996, and never once won. The runner-up mark is seven nominations, held jointly by ten other actors — among them Steve Carell, still active on the list after a seventh loss in 2026, and Larry David, nominated across three different decades for the same role.

How this was built, and where it breaks

Twelve categories, one classifier

The source CSV spells the same category more than a dozen ways per family across 76 years, and its own canonical_category column doesn’t canonicalize any of it — 364 distinct strings survive there. Categories here were regrouped from the raw text by keyword (billing: lead/supporting; gender: actor/actress; genre: drama/comedy/limited & miniseries & movie & special & anthology), which is what makes the twelve families on this page comparable at all.

Why the data starts in 1979, not 1949

Running that classifier back to the Emmys’ start turns up years where a single category shows more than one declared winner — not real ties, but the mid-1970s Emmys’ own chaos of concurrent single-performance and continuing-performance schemes, one-off category names, and in 1959, entries like “An Evening with Fred Astaire” and men nominated for “Supporting Actress.” Every year from 1979 on was checked the same way and comes back clean, aside from a handful of genuine, documented ties (1995’s Supporting Actress in a Miniseries or Special split between Judy Davis and Shirley Knight is real, not a bug). So the count starts at 1979, the year the modern six-category structure settles.

People are matched by exact name

A career is every nomination under one spelling of a name in one category family. A name change — marriage, billing — would split a career into two and understate a streak; none of the leaderboard names here are known to have one, but the dataset doesn’t carry a person ID reliable enough to check automatically (only 10% of rows have one at all).

Source: emmys-data, a compiled Primetime Emmy nominations CSV covering 1949–2026 (8,549 rows). No licence file is published with it; the underlying facts — who was nominated, who won, in what year, for the Television Academy’s own awards — are historical record of a public competition, not anyone’s creative expression.
